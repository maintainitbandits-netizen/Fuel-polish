How to Tell If Your Diesel Fuel Has Algae Growth

Published June 2026 | 5 min read

Diesel fuel algae — technically, microbial contamination — is one of the most common and costly problems facing diesel engine owners in Florida. The warm, humid climate creates the ideal breeding ground for bacteria, fungi, and yeast in your fuel tank.

Fast Fact: Up to 90% of diesel fuel system failures are caused by contaminated fuel. Regular fuel polishing can prevent nearly all of them.

What Is Diesel Fuel Algae?

"Diesel algae" isn't actually algae. It's a combination of bacteria, fungi, and yeast that live in the water layer at the bottom of your fuel tank. These microorganisms feed on diesel fuel and multiply rapidly in warm conditions — exactly what Florida provides year-round.

5 Warning Signs of Diesel Algae

1. Clogged Fuel Filters

If you're changing fuel filters more frequently than usual — or finding black, slimy residue on old filters — you likely have microbial contamination. This is often the first sign owners notice.

2. Black Sludge in Your Fuel Tank

When you inspect your tank or fuel system, a dark, slimy substance indicates active microbial growth. This sludge clogs fuel lines, injectors, and pumps.

3. Fuel Injector Problems

Rough idling, poor acceleration, black smoke, or decreased fuel economy can all be symptoms of contaminated fuel reaching your injectors.

4. Water in Your Fuel

Water is essential for microbial growth. If you find water in your fuel-water separator or at the bottom of your tank, you're at high risk for algae contamination.

5. Foul-Smelling Fuel

Diesel fuel contaminated with microbes often develops a sour, sulfur-like smell. If your fuel smells different than usual, have it tested.
